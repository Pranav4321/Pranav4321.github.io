DIVA

Depth-intelligent navigation for the visually impaired. A wearable that turns stereo depth into touch and object detection into spatial audio, so you can feel where things are and hear what they are.

Overview

The white cane only reads the ground a step ahead, and the electronic aids that do more cost more than most people who need them can pay. DIVA is built for about $278.

You wear a headband camera and hold a small pad. Anything close enough to walk into rises as a bump on the pad, in the same spot it sits in front of you. Earbuds name the objects around you, and each name comes from the object's direction. It's sensory substitution: sight is handed to touch and hearing.

How it works

Two paths run off the same headband. One turns depth into touch, and the other turns objects into sound.

Touch where things are

  1. See in stereoOAK-D LiteTwo lenses, two views. Near things shift more.
  2. Measure depthDisparity → depthThat shift becomes a distance per pixel.
  3. Grid and thresholdRaspberry PiAverage 5×5 cells; anything too close is blocked.
  4. Feel itBluetooth → ESP32 → relaysBlocked cells lift as bumps on the palm.

Sound what they are

  1. DetectYOLO on the RGB frameFinds and names objects, 80 COCO classes.
  2. LocateDepth lookupEach object's average point gets a distance.
  3. Say itHRTF spatial audioThe name plays from the object's direction.

Depth sensing

Like two eyes, the OAK-D Lite sees the room from two points a few centimetres apart. Near objects shift more between the two images than far ones, and the camera turns that shift (disparity) into a distance for every pixel.

The Raspberry Pi splits each depth frame into a 5×5 grid and averages each cell into one number. Cells closer than the wearer's threshold, set on the LCD, are marked blocked. The Pi and LCD share one case on the chest, cabled to the headband.

Tactile feedback

The grid goes over Bluetooth to the hand unit, a separate pad of 25 electromagnetic actuators under silicone. It maps 1:1 onto the palm, so top-left in front of you is top-left on your hand.

An ESP32 receives each grid and switches the actuators through relays, and a powered cell pushes up a bump you can feel. Over three builds the whole system went from 6.84 lb to 2.78 lb, and the parts came to $149 for the stereo camera, $73 for the controller and $56 for the tactile array.

Detection and audio

Touch tells you where something is, and sound tells you what it is. A lightweight YOLO model trained on COCO runs on the RGB frame and finds objects across 80 classes. The average point of each detection is looked up in the depth map to get the object's distance and direction.

The label is then rendered with head-related transfer functions (HRTFs), which shape the sound the way your head and ears would. "Chair" heard on your left means the chair is on your left.

Evaluation

  • Depth accuracy: three objects at 18 distances from 0.5 to 5 m gave a 3.4% mean error.
  • Reading the pad: blind participants identified which of 25 cells fired 21.8 times out of 25 on average, and blindfolded sighted participants 17.7.
  • Navigation: 18 participants (9 blind, 9 blindfolded) crossed a ten-obstacle indoor course with no aid, with a white cane, and with DIVA. DIVA runs were also about 10 s faster than cane runs.
